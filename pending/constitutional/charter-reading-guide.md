# Charter Reading Guide (Plain Language)

*A companion to [charter.md](charter.md) for non-lawyers. This guide is **not** part of the Charter and has no legal force — if anything here seems to differ from the Charter, the Charter wins. Its only job is to help you understand **why each section exists** and what it is doing.*

---

## How to read the Charter

Think of the Charter as the DAO's **constitution**. It does not contain the fine operational detail (how many votes, how many days, who clicks what) — that lives in the **policy library** and the **DAO Parameters Registry**. The Charter sets the *principles, the structure of authority, and the handful of rules that can never be quietly changed*. Everything else is built on top of it.

A useful mental model:

- **Sections 1–3** answer *"What is this, and what does it stand for?"*
- **Sections 4–4A** answer *"Who is in charge, and how does power switch on?"*
- **Sections 5–9** answer *"How does it actually run day to day?"*
- **Sections 10–11** answer *"Whose money is this, and what happens if it ends?"*
- **Sections 12–14** answer *"How does this document change, and how does it connect to the law?"*

## One-sentence purpose of each section

### The foundations — what this is and what it values

| § | Section | In one sentence |
|---|---------|-----------------|
| 1 | **Purpose** | States *why the DAO exists* — to put the Radix ecosystem in the hands of its token holders rather than any single company. |
| 2 | **Nature of the DAO** | Explains *what kind of thing the DAO is* — a community-run coordination system where authority stays with the community and is only *lent* to the bodies that carry out work. |
| 3 | **Core Principles** | Lists the *seven binding values* (decentralization, transparency, accountability, stewardship, resilience, integrity, environmental responsibility) that every decision and every officeholder must respect. |

*Why these are here:* before you can write rules, you have to say what the rules are *for*. These sections are the yardstick used to judge whether any later action is legitimate.

### Who holds power — authority and the switch-on moment

| § | Section | In one sentence |
|---|---------|-----------------|
| 4.1 | **Token Holder Sovereignty** | Confirms that *token holders are the ultimate authority* and points to the Operating Agreement for the exact moment their votes become legally binding. |
| 4.2 | **Established Bodies** | Lets the DAO *create accountable bodies and roles* (the Accountability Council, Working Groups, Committees, Delegates) without ever handing them ownership of the DAO. |
| 4.3 | **Binding Decisions** | Says that *once a decision is legally binding, it must actually be carried out* through the proper execution channels (and that before activation, most outcomes are advisory only). |
| 4A | **Activation and the Advisory Governance Period** | Sets up the *two-phase life of the DAO* — a member-managed start-up phase where votes are advisory, flipping to full community governance on "Activation Date." |
| 4A.1 | The Advisory Governance Period | Defines the *early phase* where community votes are advice the Transition Council must publicly consider but is not yet bound by. |
| 4A.2 | The three binding community acts | Names the *only three votes that truly bind* during the early phase: ratifying the framework, electing the Permanent Council, and the Activation Vote. |
| 4A.3 | Activation Conditions | Points to the *checklist that must be completed* before the DAO can switch on. |
| 4A.4 | Effect of Activation | Describes *what changes at the flip of the switch* — votes become binding, the temporary council retires, and the permanent one takes over. |

*Why this is here:* a brand-new DAO can't safely be fully autonomous on day one (the entity has to be formed, assets moved, a council elected). Section 4A is the carefully staged "ignition sequence" that gets from a hand-built start-up to genuine community control.

### How it runs day to day

| § | Section | In one sentence |
|---|---------|-----------------|
| 5 | **Governance System** | Describes *how decisions get made* — propose, discuss, vote, execute — and that you can vote yourself or delegate your vote. Delegation is a Charter right but is **not enabled at launch**, pending platform support (DAO Parameters §8). |
| 6 | **Treasury Stewardship** | Sets the *rules for the DAO's money* — it must serve the ecosystem, and big spends need a governance vote. |
| 7 | **Governance Safeguards** | Guarantees there are *brakes and emergency tools* to stop harmful actions, settle disputes, and report misconduct by people in power. |
| 8 | **Governance Integrity** | Establishes the *Accountability Council as a referee* that makes sure the process is followed — without itself becoming a ruler — and requires officials to disclose conflicts. |
| 9 | **Continuity and Fallback** | Promises the DAO *keeps working even if things break*, with power falling back to token holders, while still allowing a lawful shutdown. |

*Why these are here:* principles are worthless if there's no machinery to apply them. These sections are the engine room — the actual mechanisms for deciding, spending, policing, and surviving.

### Whose assets these are — the non-profit guarantees

| § | Section | In one sentence |
|---|---------|-----------------|
| 10 | **Non-Distribution Principle** | Locks in that the *DAO's money cannot be paid out to enrich its participants* — it's a steward, not a profit-sharing vehicle. |
| 11 | **Asset Lock and Wind-Down** | Guarantees that *if the DAO ever closes, leftover assets go to a similar-purpose organisation*, never to members or token holders. |

*Why these are here:* these two are the heart of the DAO's **non-profit** legal status. Take them away and the DAO stops being a public-benefit steward and becomes something a regulator would treat very differently — which is exactly why §12 makes them un-removable.

### How the document changes and connects to the law

| § | Section | In one sentence |
|---|---------|-----------------|
| 12 | **Amendments** | Explains *how the Charter can be changed* — only through the highest-bar "Constitutional" vote, because changing the constitution should be hard. |
| 12.1 | **Entrenched Provisions** | Lists the *rules that can never be changed by any vote* — the non-profit guarantees, the minimum quorum floor, the ban on anyone overriding a valid vote, and the protected voting-power sources. |
| 12.2 | **Emergency Amendments** | Provides a *narrow, temporary "break-glass" power* to patch a policy in a crisis, which automatically expires unless the community ratifies it within 30 days. |
| 13 | **Scope** | Defines *where the Charter sits in the pecking order* — below Marshall Islands law and the Operating Agreement, but above every operational policy. |
| 14 | **Notices and Service** | Sets the *official addresses for being contacted* — legal mail goes to the Registered Agent; community notices are published to the Official Venue — and requires legal mail to be shared with the community. |

*Why these are here:* a constitution has to say how it evolves and how it plugs into the real legal world. §12 protects it from rushed or self-serving edits, §13 prevents conflicts by setting a clear hierarchy, and §14 makes sure messages (including a lawsuit) actually reach the right people.

---

## If you only remember three things

1. **Power belongs to token holders.** Every body and role is borrowing authority, never owning it (§§2, 4.1).
2. **Some rules are permanent.** The non-profit guarantees and core governance floors cannot be voted away (§§10, 11, 12.1).
3. **The Charter is the constitution, not the rulebook.** The day-to-day numbers and procedures live in the policies and the Parameters Registry — the Charter sets the principles they must obey (§13).
